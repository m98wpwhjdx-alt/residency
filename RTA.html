1. What RTA means in the ICU

RTA is a renal tubular failure of acid handling with relatively preserved glomerular filtration. In critical care it appears as persistent hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis that does not behave like lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, renal failure, or diarrheal bicarbonate loss.

The acid–base phenotype

Low HCO₃⁻Normal AGHigh Cl⁻

Confirm with pH/PaCO₂ and calculate corrected anion gap. Mixed disorders are common in shock, sepsis, DKA recovery, and saline resuscitation.

The renal question

Can the kidney excrete NH₄⁺?

Appropriate response to metabolic acidosis is increased ammonium excretion. RTA implies an inappropriately low renal acid excretion response.

The ICU context

DrugsAKI/CKDObstructionAutoimmune disease

Medication and renal perfusion context often matter more than elegant textbook labels.

Bedside anchor: Do not diagnose RTA from bicarbonate alone. First prove metabolic acidosis, correct the anion gap for albumin, and exclude common non-renal bicarbonate loss.

6. ICU management framework

Immediate priorities

ECG + K⁺ riskpH severityVentilatory compensation

Stabilize severe hyperkalemia first. In severe hypokalemia, replete potassium before giving large bicarbonate doses. Check magnesium.

Remove the driver

Stop or substitute offenders when possible: amphotericin B, acetazolamide/topiramate, ACEi/ARB, MR antagonists, heparin, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, calcineurin inhibitors.

Alkali therapy

Use sodium bicarbonate or potassium citrate depending on potassium status and stone risk. Proximal RTA may require higher doses than distal RTA because bicarbonate is wasted.

Type 4-specific moves

Reduce potassium intake/load, stop K⁺-raising drugs, use loop/thiazide diuretics if volume status allows, consider fludrocortisone in selected hypoaldosterone states, and use potassium binders when appropriate.

Bicarbonate is not benign: sodium load, volume expansion, CO₂ generation, lower ionized calcium, and potassium shifts can matter in ventilated or shock patients.

7. Pitfalls and cognitive forcing functions

Pitfall 1: Calling every NAGMA “RTA” ＋
NAGMA is a pattern, not a diagnosis. Always check GI bicarbonate loss, chloride-rich fluids, renal failure, drugs, and recovery from organic acidosis.
Pitfall 2: Over-trusting urine pH ＋
Urine pH is altered by UTI with urease-producing organisms, low urine sodium delivery, volume depletion, bicarbonaturia, and sample handling. It must be interpreted with serum pH/HCO₃⁻ and K⁺.
Pitfall 3: Misusing urine anion gap ＋
UAG is a surrogate for urinary NH₄⁺ only when NH₄⁺ is primarily excreted with chloride. Ketoacids, hippurate from toluene, penicillin anions, or advanced kidney dysfunction may confound it. Consider urine osmolal gap where available.
Pitfall 4: Missing mixed acid–base disease ＋
Hypoalbuminemia can mask a high anion gap. Delta-gap reasoning and albumin correction are essential in sepsis, liver disease, burns, malnutrition, and post-operative critical illness.
Pitfall 5: Worsening hypokalemia with alkali ＋
In distal/proximal RTA with severe hypokalemia, alkali therapy can move potassium intracellularly and increase urinary potassium losses. Replace K⁺ early and repeatedly monitor.
