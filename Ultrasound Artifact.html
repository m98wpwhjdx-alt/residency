Ultrasound Artifacts

Artifacts are not random errors. They are predictable image lies created when the ultrasound machine relies on simplified assumptions about sound. This page explains each major artifact using animated B-mode style simulations.

The 5 false assumptions

The machine converts time and echo intensity into pixels. Artifacts appear when real tissue violates these assumptions.

Constant speed

The scanner assumes sound travels at about 1540 m/s in soft tissue, but fat, muscle, bone, and fluid all differ.

Straight lines

The scanner assumes pulses travel straight, but refraction bends sound at oblique boundaries.

Echo returns inline

The scanner assumes returning echoes come back along the same beam line, not from off-axis paths.

Single reflection

The scanner assumes one reflection per pulse, but sound can bounce repeatedly between strong reflectors.

Linear attenuation

The scanner assumes weakening is mainly depth-dependent, but bone, air, fluid, and calcification attenuate very differently.

1. Refraction artifacts

Sound bends when it crosses a tissue boundary at an angle and enters a medium with a different propagation speed.

Refraction — duplication & edge shadow
Mechanism

Object duplication and edge shadowing

Object duplication: at an oblique tissue interface, the beam bends and reaches a real lesion via a side path. The scanner assumes the echo came back along the original straight beam line (dashed), so it plots a duplicate copy in the wrong location.

Edge shadowing: at the curved edge of a cyst or vessel, the beam strikes obliquely, refracts away, and leaves a wedge-shaped region that receives little sound — visible as a dark stripe extending beyond the lateral edge.

sin θ_t / sin θ_i = c₂ / c₁
Clinical clue: shadow starts from the lateral edge, not from the whole object.
Fix: change probe angle or scan from another window.

2. Mirror artifact

A strong reflector forces sound onto an indirect extra path. The added travel time is misread as added depth.

Mirror
Mechanism

Object appears on the opposite side of a reflector

The pulse strikes the lesion, then bounces off a strong, smooth interface (diaphragm, pleura, bladder wall) before returning to the probe.

Because the machine uses only return time and assumes a straight path, it places a duplicate of the lesion deeper, behind the reflector.

strong reflectorextra travel timeduplicate beyond boundary
Classic example: liver lesion mirrored above the diaphragm, mimicking lung pathology.

3. Reverberation

Multiple reflections between strong reflectors create repeated horizontal echoes at progressively greater apparent depth.

Reverberation
Mechanism

Repeated echoes at increasing depth

Sound bounces back and forth between the transducer and a strong reflector (or between two strong reflectors). Each delayed echo is interpreted as a separate, deeper interface.

The result is a stack of parallel bright lines. Spacing is roughly equal because each bounce adds a similar extra path length, and brightness decreases with depth as energy is lost.

multiple reflectionsparallel linesfades with depth
Clinical clue: equally-spaced bright horizontal lines, dimming with depth — common with metallic surfaces, pleura, and bowel gas.

4. Ring-down and comet-tail

Both create bright vertical streaks descending from a tiny strong reflector — a hallmark of gas, metal, or calcification.

Ring-down / comet-tail
Mechanism

Continuous or closely spaced echoes

Ring-down: fluid trapped between gas microbubbles resonates and emits a continuous train of echoes, producing a bright, solid vertical streak deep to the source.

Comet-tail: tightly packed reverberation between two small, closely spaced, highly reflective surfaces — for example metallic surgical clips or cholesterol crystals.

vertical streakgas or metalhigh reflectivity
Clinical clue: B-lines in lung ultrasound (interstitial syndrome), pneumobilia, surgical clips, adenomyomatosis of the gallbladder.

5. Side lobe / grating lobe artifact

Energy outside the main beam reflects off an off-axis object, but the scanner places it inside the main image.

Side lobe + beam diagram
Mechanism

Off-axis echoes plotted on-axis

The transducer emits a main beam plus smaller side lobes (and grating lobes in array probes — shown in green on the right). When a side lobe travels off-axis and strikes a strong reflector — bowel gas, a wire, a calcified wall — the echo returns to the transducer along the same off-axis path.

The machine has no way to know the echo came from the side. It assumes every returning echo arrived from the main beam axis, so it plots the off-axis object at the same depth, but on the central axis — typically appearing as a curved bright streak inside an anechoic space (bladder, cyst, gallbladder).

off-axis reflectorbowel gasfalse internal echoes
Clinical clue: spurious curved echoes inside fluid-filled spaces. Reduce gain, change probe angle, or use harmonic imaging to confirm.

6. Shadowing and enhancement

The machine expects predictable weakening of sound with depth. Some structures attenuate too much; some too little.

Shadow vs enhancement
Mechanism

Posterior shadowing and posterior enhancement

Shadowing: bone, calcification, stone, or gas attenuates or reflects most of the beam. Tissue behind it receives little sound and appears dark — a clean wedge extending from the structure to the bottom of the screen.

Enhancement: fluid attenuates less than surrounding tissue. The deeper tissue receives a stronger beam than the machine's TGC expects, so it looks brighter than expected.

stone = shadowfluid = enhancementdepth-gain assumption fails
Clinical clue: posterior enhancement supports cystic / fluid content; clean shadow supports calcification, stone, bone, or gas.

7. Speed error

The scanner uses echo return time to calculate depth. Wrong speed means wrong depth.

Speed error — line displacement
Mechanism

Step-off at a deep linear boundary

The machine assumes an average soft-tissue speed of 1540 m/s. Sound passing through a structure of different speed returns at the wrong time, and the scanner plots the deeper interface at the wrong depth.

The classic appearance is a smooth horizontal line (e.g. diaphragm, posterior bladder wall) that kinks down directly behind a slower lesion, because echoes from that column took longer than expected and were plotted deeper.

time-of-flight errorslower medium → deeper plotstep-off appearance
Depth = assumed speed × return time ÷ 2
Wrong assumed speed → wrong displayed depth.

8. Range ambiguity

Delayed echoes from deep structures return during the next listening window and are displayed superficially.

Range ambiguity
Mechanism

Deep echo arrives too late

For superficial imaging the scanner uses short pulse-repetition intervals and short listening windows, but a previous pulse may continue deep into tissue.

If a deep echo returns during the next pulse cycle, the scanner attributes it to that newer pulse and plots it in the superficial field — a ghost that does not belong there.

late returnrange ambiguitysuperficial ghost
Clinical clue: a ghost-like structure appears too shallow and may disappear after reducing PRF or increasing depth.

One-line summary

Ultrasound artifacts are diagnostic clues produced by predictable violations of machine assumptions: constant speed, straight-line travel, inline echo return, single reflection, and linear attenuation. Mastering them helps you avoid false diagnoses and use artifacts clinically — especially in lung ultrasound, FAST, biliary scans, vascular scans, and ICU bedside imaging.
