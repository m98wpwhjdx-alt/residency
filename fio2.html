Flow and FiO₂ Relationship in ICU

A bedside guide to understand how oxygen flow, patient inspiratory demand, and device type determine the actual FiO₂ delivered to the patient.

1. Core Concept

FiO₂ means the fraction of inspired oxygen reaching the patient. Room air has an FiO₂ of approximately 21%, while pure oxygen source gas is close to 100%.

The actual FiO₂ depends on how much oxygen mixes with room air before reaching the alveoli.

The most important determinant is whether the device flow can meet the patient’s peak inspiratory flow demand.

Low device flow
Device flow < patient inspiratory demand

Room air gets entrained. Actual FiO₂ becomes variable and often lower than expected.

High device flow
Device flow ≥ patient inspiratory demand

Room air entrainment decreases. Delivered FiO₂ becomes more stable and predictable.

2. Low-Flow Oxygen Systems

Examples include nasal cannula and simple face mask. These devices do not meet the complete inspiratory flow demand of most ICU patients, so room air is entrained.

Nasal Cannula Approximation

Oxygen Flow Approximate FiO₂ Bedside Interpretation
1 L/min 24% Mild oxygen supplementation
2 L/min 28% Common starting flow
3 L/min 32% Moderate support
4 L/min 36% Higher low-flow support
5 L/min 40% Near upper comfortable limit
6 L/min 44% Maximum usual nasal cannula range
Clinical caution: This “+4% per L/min” rule is only approximate. FiO₂ falls if the patient has tachypnea, high tidal volume, high inspiratory flow, or mouth breathing.

3. Face Mask and Non-Rebreather Mask

Device Usual Flow Approximate FiO₂ Important Point
Simple Face Mask 5–10 L/min 35–60% Do not use below 5 L/min because of risk of CO₂ rebreathing.
Non-Rebreather Mask 10–15 L/min 60–90% or higher Reservoir bag should remain inflated during inspiration.
If the non-rebreather reservoir bag collapses during inspiration, oxygen flow is inadequate for the patient’s inspiratory demand.

4. Venturi Mask

A Venturi mask is a fixed-performance oxygen device. Here, the selected FiO₂ is controlled by the Venturi adaptor, not simply by increasing oxygen flow.

In a Venturi system, oxygen flow must be set according to the manufacturer’s instruction for that FiO₂ adaptor.
Concept Meaning
FiO₂ Fixed by the Venturi adaptor, e.g., 24%, 28%, 35%, 40%, 60%.
Flow Must be sufficient to drive the Venturi effect and total gas delivery.
Best use COPD or patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure where controlled oxygen is needed.

In many COPD patients at risk of CO₂ retention, the usual SpO₂ target is 88–92%, unless otherwise clinically indicated.

5. HFNC / HFNO

High-flow nasal cannula allows independent adjustment of:

  • Flow: commonly up to 60 L/min in adults.
  • FiO₂: usually 21% to 100%.
  • Temperature and humidification: improves comfort and secretion clearance.
Knob Main Effect Clinical Meaning
Increase FiO₂ Increases oxygen concentration Useful when SpO₂/PaO₂ is low despite adequate flow.
Increase Flow Reduces room air entrainment, washes out dead space, gives small PEEP effect Useful when patient has high inspiratory demand or work of breathing.
In HFNC, FiO₂ controls oxygen concentration, while flow controls how reliably that FiO₂ is delivered and how much respiratory support is provided.

Example

A patient on HFNC FiO₂ 0.60, flow 30 L/min may still desaturate if their inspiratory demand is 60–80 L/min. Increasing flow to 50–60 L/min may improve oxygenation even without increasing FiO₂ because less room air is entrained.

6. Mechanical Ventilator

On an invasive ventilator, FiO₂ is set directly by the oxygen-air blender. For example: FiO₂ 0.30, 0.50, 0.80, or 1.0.

Inspiratory flow mainly affects respiratory mechanics, not oxygen concentration.

Ventilator Setting Main Effect
FiO₂ Directly changes inspired oxygen concentration.
Inspiratory Flow Affects inspiratory time, I:E ratio, peak pressure, patient comfort, and synchrony.
PEEP Improves alveolar recruitment and oxygenation, especially in shunt physiology.
On a ventilator, increasing inspiratory flow does not usually increase FiO₂. FiO₂ is adjusted separately.

7. Bedside Summary Table

System Flow–FiO₂ Relationship Practical ICU Point
Nasal Cannula Higher flow gives higher approximate FiO₂, but variable. Good for stable patients with low oxygen requirement.
Simple Mask Higher flow increases FiO₂, but still variable. Minimum 5 L/min to reduce CO₂ rebreathing.
Non-Rebreather Needs high flow to maintain reservoir and high FiO₂. Use 10–15 L/min; bag should not collapse.
Venturi Mask FiO₂ fixed by adaptor; flow must be set correctly. Best for controlled oxygen therapy, especially COPD.
HFNC FiO₂ and flow are independently set. Increase flow for high inspiratory demand/work of breathing.
Mechanical Ventilator FiO₂ is set directly; flow affects mechanics. For oxygenation, adjust FiO₂ and PEEP, not inspiratory flow alone.

8. One-Line ICU Takeaway

Low-flow oxygen: Flow partly determines FiO₂.
HFNC: Flow and FiO₂ are separate but clinically linked.
Ventilator: FiO₂ is directly set; flow mainly affects mechanics.

Educational ICU note | Use clinical targets such as SpO₂, ABG, work of breathing, and underlying pathology to titrate oxygen support.
