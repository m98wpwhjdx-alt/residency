Estimating RVSP and PASP Using the Tricuspid Regurgitation Jet

A practical ICU-oriented guide to estimating right ventricular systolic pressure and, by inference, pulmonary artery systolic pressure using continuous-wave Doppler echocardiography.

ICU framing

What question are we asking?

The tricuspid regurgitation jet provides a non-invasive way of asking: “What pressure is the RV generating during systole?”

If there is no right ventricular outflow tract obstruction or pulmonic stenosis, right ventricular systolic pressure approximates pulmonary artery systolic pressure.

1. Core physiology

During systole, if tricuspid regurgitation is present, blood flows backward from the right ventricle into the right atrium. The velocity of that regurgitant jet reflects the pressure gradient between the RV and RA.

ΔPRV-RA = 4V²

Where V is the peak TR velocity in m/s.

RVSP = 4(TRV)² + RAP
PASP ≈ RVSP

This is the simplified Bernoulli approach used in echo estimation of pulmonary artery systolic pressure.

2. Why RAP is added

The TR jet gives only the pressure gradient between the RV and RA.

Example: Peak TR velocity = 3.0 m/s

4 × 3² = 36 mmHg

This means RV systolic pressure is 36 mmHg above right atrial pressure.

If estimated RAP is 10 mmHg:

RVSP = 36 + 10 = 46 mmHg

Estimated PASP is about 46 mmHg, assuming no RVOT obstruction.

3. Estimating RAP from the IVC

Echo commonly estimates RAP from IVC diameter and inspiratory collapsibility. A classic ASE-style framework is:

IVC findingEstimated RAP
IVC ≤ 2.1 cm and collapses > 50% with sniff~3 mmHg
IVC > 2.1 cm and collapses < 50%~15 mmHg
Intermediate findings~8 mmHg
ICU cautionIn ventilated ICU patients, IVC-derived RAP is a rough hemodynamic estimate, not a precise number. Mechanical ventilation, high PEEP, elevated intra-abdominal pressure, RV dysfunction, severe TR, and poor inspiratory effort can distort interpretation.

4. Worked example: mild elevation

TRV = 2.6 m/s
RAP = 8 mmHg

RVSP = 4(2.6)² + 8
RVSP = 4(6.76) + 8
RVSP = 27 + 8 = 35 mmHg

Estimated PASP ≈ 35 mmHg.

This is borderline or mildly elevated, depending on age, clinical setting, and other echo probability markers.

Worked example: significant PH signal

TRV = 3.8 m/s
RAP = 15 mmHg

RVSP = 4(3.8)² + 15
RVSP = 4(14.44) + 15
RVSP = 57.8 + 15 = 72.8 mmHg

Estimated PASP ≈ 73 mmHg.

This is a high RV systolic pressure and may strongly suggest significant pulmonary hypertension or acute RV afterload elevation in the right clinical context.

5. How to acquire the TR signal properly

For bedside echo, acquisition error is a major source of misclassification.

  • Use continuous-wave Doppler through the TR jet.
  • Try multiple windows: apical 4-chamber, parasternal RV inflow, parasternal short-axis, subcostal 4-chamber, and modified low parasternal views.
  • Align the Doppler cursor as parallel as possible to the regurgitant jet.
  • Measure peak velocity at the outer edge of the densest envelope.
  • Avoid measuring faint noise outside the true spectral envelope.
  • Measure at end-expiration when feasible, especially in ventilated patients.
  • In atrial fibrillation, average several representative beats.
  • With marked respiratory variation, note the ventilatory phase.
A good TR signal is:Dense enough to trace confidently, complete with a clear systolic envelope, and reproducible over several beats.

6. Common ICU pitfalls

A. Poor TR envelope

Poor alignment or an incomplete envelope underestimates PASP. No measurable TR jet does not exclude pulmonary hypertension.

B. Severe TR

With severe or free TR, RV and RA pressures may equalize early. A triangular, low-velocity envelope may underestimate RVSP.

C. RV failure

A failing RV may be unable to generate high pressure. A modest TRV in shock with a dilated, poorly functioning RV is not reassuring.

D. Mechanical ventilation

PEEP, dynamic hyperinflation, high mean airway pressure, and increased intrathoracic pressure affect venous return and IVC behavior.

E. RVOT obstruction

RVSP equals PASP only if there is no significant gradient between the RV and pulmonary artery.

Key implication

RVSP ≠ PASP

In pulmonic stenosis, RVOT obstruction, or conduit obstruction, RVSP may be high while PA pressure is not equivalently high.

7. Severity interpretation

Estimated PASP/RVSPInterpretation
~35–45 mmHgMild elevation
~46–60 mmHgModerate elevation
>60 mmHgSevere elevation
Guideline-style interpretationEcho should estimate the probability of pulmonary hypertension rather than diagnose it from PASP alone. TR velocity should be interpreted alongside RV size/function, septal flattening, pulmonary artery size, RA size, IVC/RAP, RVOT acceleration time, and clinical context. Right-heart catheterization is required when definitive diagnosis is needed.

8. ICU bedside interpretation

Acute PE

You may see RV dilation, McConnell-type regional dysfunction, septal flattening, elevated TRV, and a dilated IVC. But if the RV is crashing, RVSP may not be very high because the RV cannot generate pressure indefinitely.

ARDS or hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction

PASP may rise due to hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis, high PEEP, overdistension, pulmonary microthrombosis, or vasoconstriction. Management may include optimizing oxygenation, pH, lung-protective ventilation, avoiding excessive driving pressure or PEEP, and considering pulmonary vasodilators in selected RV failure physiology.

Left heart disease

Elevated PASP with LA enlargement, LV systolic or diastolic dysfunction, MR, or high E/e′ suggests post-capillary PH. The initial treatment target is left-sided filling pressure and the underlying cardiac lesion, not pulmonary vasodilation.

Sepsis

RV dysfunction in sepsis may coexist with elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, high ventilatory pressures, myocardial depression, and volume overload. RVSP alone is less useful than the full RV phenotype: size, systolic function, septal position, TAPSE, S′, FAC, TR severity, and venous congestion.

9. Practical bedside checklist

When you see a reported RVSP or PASP, ask:

  1. Was the TR envelope adequate?
  2. What was the actual TR velocity?
  3. What RAP was assumed?
  4. Is the patient spontaneously breathing or ventilated?
  5. Is there severe TR?
  6. Is there RVOT obstruction or pulmonic stenosis?
  7. Is the RV dilated or failing?
  8. Is the septum flattened in systole, diastole, or both?
  9. Does the clinical scenario suggest acute versus chronic RV afterload?
  10. Would this result change management enough to need formal echo or right-heart catheterization?

10. Bottom line

The key equation is:

RVSP/PASP = 4(TRV)² + RAP

It is powerful because TR velocity is a surrogate for the RV–RA systolic gradient. But in the ICU, the number is only as good as the Doppler envelope, RAP estimate, and physiologic assumptions.
