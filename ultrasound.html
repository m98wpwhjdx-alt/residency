Foundation

1.Acoustic impedance

Acoustic impedance determines how much of an ultrasound wave is reflected back to the probe versus transmitted onward. It is the single most important property for understanding why we see what we see.

Definition

Z = ρ × c

Acoustic impedance (Z) is the opposition tissue offers to ultrasound propagation. It depends on tissue density (ρ) and the speed of sound (c) in that tissue.

Z = ρ × c Units: rayl (kg · m⁻² · s⁻¹). Higher density or higher sound speed → higher impedance.

Reflection

Intensity reflection coefficient

R = ((Z₂ − Z₁) / (Z₂ + Z₁))² Valid at perpendicular (normal) incidence. A large mismatch → near-total reflection.

Transmission coefficient: T = 1 − R. Air–tissue and bone–tissue interfaces reflect >99% of the beam, which is why both produce strong shadows.

Tissue values (approx.)

MediumZ (× 10⁶ rayl)Behaviour
Air0.0004Total reflection
Fat1.34Good window
Water1.48Reference / gel
Soft tissue1.63Diagnostic
Bone7.8Reflects + shadows

Wave at an interface

Incident, reflected, transmitted

Clinical translation: Reflection is highest when the beam is perpendicular to the interface. Tilt and rotate the probe until the pleural line, vessel wall or needle becomes brightest. Off-axis incidence sends echoes away from the probe → dim image.

Acoustic windows

Why we love fluid

  • Air: Z ≈ 0.0004 — almost total reflection at any soft-tissue interface.
  • Bone: high Z plus very high attenuation — bright surface, hard shadow beyond.
  • Fluid (urine, bile, blood, effusion): low attenuation, low scatter → anechoic, deep penetration.

Use a full bladder, a pleural effusion or the liver as natural windows to see structures behind them.

Beam–tissue interactions

2.What happens to the beam

Reflection is only one of four things the beam does. Understanding all four explains shadowing, posterior enhancement, edge artefact and dropout — the daily artefact vocabulary of bedside ultrasound.

The four interactions

InteractionWhat you see
Reflection (specular)Bright lines from smooth interfaces (pleura, diaphragm).
ScatteringSpeckle / parenchymal texture (liver, spleen, muscle).
RefractionEdge shadow at curved interfaces (gallbladder edge).
Attenuation (absorption + scatter + reflection)Image fades with depth — needs TGC compensation.

Common artefacts at a glance

ArtefactCause
Acoustic shadowingStrong reflector (bone, gas, calculus) blocks the beam.
Posterior acoustic enhancementFluid attenuates less → tissue beyond looks brighter.
Reverberation (e.g. A-lines)Beam bounces between two strong reflectors.
Mirror imageCurved reflector (diaphragm) duplicates structure beyond it.
Comet tail / B-lineTightly spaced micro-reflectors with low attenuation.
Lung ultrasound

3.Lung ultrasound — read the artefacts

Air has near-zero acoustic impedance, so the pleural surface reflects most of the beam. Lung ultrasound therefore reads artefacts at the pleura, not deep parenchyma. The handful of patterns below answer most ICU questions about dyspnoea.

M-mode

4.M-mode signs at the pleura

M-mode plots a single ultrasound line over time. At the pleura it converts the presence or absence of sliding into a pattern that is unambiguous on a still image — useful for documentation and for confirming pneumothorax.

Seashore sign — normal lung

Linear waves above the pleura (motionless soft tissue) and granular "sand" below (sliding lung) → seashore sign.

Barcode / stratosphere sign — pneumothorax

Linear pattern both above and below the pleural line — no sliding. Stratosphere / barcode sign.
Lung point is the gold-standard sign of pneumothorax — the visualised transition between the seashore (sliding) and barcode (no sliding) patterns within a single intercostal space. It is highly specific but has low sensitivity in large pneumothoraces, where sliding is absent everywhere reachable.
Decision tool

5.BLUE protocol — acute dyspnoea in 3 minutes

Lichtenstein's BLUE protocol uses three pairs of zones (upper anterior, lower anterior, posterolateral PLAPS) to assign one of seven profiles. Each profile maps to a top differential with reported accuracy >90% in undifferentiated dyspnoea.

Simplified BLUE algorithm. Anterior sliding + A-lines → think PE/COPD/asthma. Bilateral B-lines → cardiogenic oedema. Absent sliding + lung point → pneumothorax.
Doppler

6.Doppler — direction and velocity

Moving red cells shift the frequency of reflected ultrasound. Flow toward the probe gives a positive shift (above baseline); flow away gives a negative shift (below baseline). The Doppler equation tells you what to optimise to get reliable velocities.

Doppler equation

Why angle matters

Δf = (2 · f₀ · v · cos θ) / c Δf = frequency shift · f₀ = transmitted frequency · v = blood velocity · θ = angle between beam and flow · c = sound speed
  • Optimal: θ < 60° (ideally near 0°) — maximal cosθ, accurate velocity.
  • θ = 90°: cosθ = 0 → no shift, no signal. (Opposite of B-mode imaging, which prefers 90°.)
  • Angle correct by aligning the cursor parallel to the visible vessel.
  • Aliasing = velocity exceeds Nyquist limit (PRF / 2). Increase scale, lower frequency, or use CW Doppler.

Spectral Doppler

Live sweep — real waveform

Above baseline → toward probe Below baseline → away from probe

Waveform height reflects velocity. Spectral broadening (filling of the envelope) indicates turbulent flow — typical of stenosis.

Quantitative Doppler

7.Vascular indices and waveform reading

Beyond peak velocity, two derived indices describe the impedance of the vascular bed downstream of the sample volume. They are central to assessing renal, cerebral and obstetric circulation.

Resistive Index

RI

RI = (PSV − EDV) / PSV PSV = peak systolic velocity · EDV = end-diastolic velocity
  • Normal renal RI ≈ 0.6 (range 0.5–0.7).
  • RI > 0.7 → high downstream resistance: AKI, renal vein congestion, obstructive uropathy, transplant rejection.
  • Very low RI (<0.5) → low resistance / arteriovenous shunting.

Pulsatility Index

PI

PI = (PSV − EDV) / TAMV TAMV = time-averaged mean velocity over the cardiac cycle
  • More sensitive than RI when EDV reaches zero or reverses (uses mean velocity, not just peak).
  • Used for middle cerebral artery (MCA) Doppler in obstetrics and TCD in raised ICP.

Bedside cheat sheet

8.ICU takeaways

A working summary you can use at the bedside on rounds.

Image acquisition

  • Use fluid as your acoustic window.
  • Avoid air and bone where possible.
  • Keep targets perpendicular to the beam in B-mode.
  • Adjust depth, focus and TGC before adjusting gain.

Lung US

  • A-lines + sliding = aerated.
  • ≥3 B-lines per scan field = interstitial syndrome.
  • Lung point = pneumothorax (highly specific).
  • Dynamic air bronchograms in consolidation favour pneumonia over atelectasis.
  • Spine sign above diaphragm = effusion.

Doppler

  • Angle < 60° for accurate velocity.
  • Optimise scale, baseline, wall filter and gain — in that order.
  • Colour first to map, PW to quantify, CW for high-velocity jets.
  • Renal RI > 0.7 → high downstream resistance.
Core rule of bedside ultrasound: image quality is determined by impedance mismatch, beam angle, attenuation and Doppler alignment. In lung ultrasound, almost everything you see is an artefact generated at the pleural interface — and that is exactly why the patterns are reproducible and clinically powerful.
